### 1. Meta / Facebook

**The failures**

- **Cambridge Analytica (2014–2018, disclosed 2018)** `[FACT]`: A personality-quiz app harvested data on ~87 million users (most of whom never installed it, via the friends-permissions API) and passed it to Cambridge Analytica for political micro-targeting, including work for the 2016 Trump campaign and Brexit-adjacent efforts. Facebook knew of the breach in 2015 `[FACT]` — it demanded certifications of data deletion that were not verified `[FACT]` — and didn't disclose to users until Guardian/Observer and NYT reporting forced it in March 2018.
- **Myanmar genocide amplification (2017–2018)** `[FACT]`: UN investigators and Facebook's own commissioned human-rights assessment (2018) found the platform was a "determining role" in inciting violence against the Rohingya, with military-linked accounts running coordinated disinformation and hate campaigns that Facebook had inadequate Burmese-language moderation capacity to catch. Reuters documented over a thousand examples of hate speech left up for years.
- **2016 election / Internet Research Agency influence operations** `[FACT]`: Russian IRA-linked pages (e.g., "Blacktivist," "Heart of Texas") reached an estimated 126 million US users with organic content plus paid ads, per Facebook's own disclosure to Congress in 2017.
- **Facebook Files / whistleblower disclosures (Frances Haugen, 2021)** `[FACT, documents]` / **internal findings interpretation** `[mix of FACT and INFERENCE]`: Internal research shown to the Wall Street Journal and Congress included: Instagram's own researchers finding the app made body-image issues worse for 1 in 3 teen girls who already felt bad about their bodies; a 2019 internal report acknowledging "we exacerbate" ethnic violence in some countries; research on "angry" reaction emoji being weighted 5x in ranking (2017–2019) that internal staff flagged as boosting "misinformation, toxicity, and low-quality news," which Meta rolled back only in 2019 after data confirmed the harm.
- **Ad discrimination / housing, employment, credit ("dark ads")** `[FACT/LITIGATION]`: ProPublica (2016) showed Facebook's ad targeting allowed exclusion by "ethnic affinity," enabling housing-discrimination-adjacent targeting; HUD filed a formal complaint in 2019 alleging Fair Housing Act violations in ad delivery algorithms (not just targeting choices — the delivery algorithm itself skewed audiences by race/gender even when advertisers targeted broadly). Settled in part in 2022 with Meta agreeing to change its ad delivery system.
- **Genocide/violence-adjacent litigation** `[ALLEGATION/LITIGATION]`: Rohingya refugees filed a $150B suit against Meta (2021, US and UK) alleging the platform's algorithm amplified hate speech that fueled the genocide. Unresolved as of cutoff.
- **Teen mental health litigation (2022–present)** `[ALLEGATION/LITIGATION]`: Dozens of US state AGs and a multidistrict litigation allege Meta designed features (infinite scroll, push notifications, "like" counts, beauty filters) to be addictive to minors while knowing the mental-health costs internally.
- **January 6 / 2020 election "Stop the Steal" ecosystem** `[FACT, with disputed causal weight]`: Internal Meta researchers found Stop the Steal groups grew explosively pre-Jan-6 via recommendation and group-join mechanics faster than enforcement could act, per Haugen documents and Meta's own "Civic Integrity" team's post-mortems, and Meta disbanded the Civic Integrity team shortly after the 2020 election `[FACT]`.

**The mechanism**

Meta's core product is attention inventory sold to advertisers; the ranking algorithm (News Feed, then Reels) optimizes for engagement proxies — clicks, comments, shares, time spent, "meaningful social interactions" (MSI, introduced 2018). `[INFERENCE, but grounded in Meta's own public statements about MSI]`: MSI was intended to boost friends-and-family content, but in practice comments and reshares correlate strongly with outrage and moral-emotional content, so optimizing for MSI systematically upweighted divisive, angering material — a mechanism Meta's own researchers documented (the 2019 "anger = 5x" finding). The friend-graph plus open group-join architecture meant harmful communities (anti-vax, election-denial, ethnic-hate groups) could scale via the same recommendation surfaces built for benign hobby groups, with moderation capacity (language coverage, local context, human reviewers) lagging growth in every market outside the US/Western Europe — this is well documented for Myanmar, Ethiopia, and India specifically `[FACT for Myanmar; broader pattern is INFERENCE from parallel reporting]`.

**The long tail**

- Post-Cambridge Analytica, "surveillance advertising" itself became a named political target, leading eventually to Apple's App Tracking Transparency (2021), which cut Meta's ad-targeting efficacy and cost the company an estimated $10B+ in 2022 revenue `[FACT]` — a self-inflicted regulatory/platform response Meta did not foresee in 2014.
- Group-based radicalization pathways identified in Facebook's own 2016 internal research ("64% of people who joined extremist groups did so because of our recommendation algorithms") `[FACT, per Haugen-leaked internal presentation reported by WSJ]` fed half a decade of civic-integrity firefighting that never structurally resolved the recommendation dynamic — it was patched market by market, election by election.
- The Instagram body-image findings, sat on internally since at least 2019, only became public via 2021 whistleblower disclosure — meaning the company had two-plus years of internal knowledge before any accountability pressure existed. This is the clearest "knew and didn't act" instance across all five platforms.

**The root cause** `[mix of FACT for the internal knowledge, INFERENCE for "root cause" framing]`

The company repeatedly measured harms (angry-reaction ranking effects, teen body image, ethnic-violence amplification, extremist-group recommendation) through credible internal research, and in most cases the intervention that would have addressed the harm (de-weighting engagement-correlated-with-outrage signals; slowing recommendation-driven group growth; restricting minors' feed algorithms) was either not shipped, shipped years late, or reversed when it hurt engagement metrics. The business model — ad revenue tied to time-on-platform and engagement density — put structural pressure against any fix that reduced those metrics, and growth/engagement KPIs sat with more organizational power than the integrity/policy teams that produced the warning research.

### 2. Instagram

**The failures**

- **Teen mental health / body image (documented internally 2019, public 2021)** `[FACT]`: The "Instagram is toxic for teen girls" research (leaked slide: "We make body image issues worse for one in three teen girls") is the single most cited document in the Facebook Files. Instagram also found teens themselves blamed Instagram for increases in anxiety and depression, in their own words, in the research.
- **"Instagram Kids" project (2021)** `[FACT]`: Meta was developing a version of Instagram for under-13s while its own research showed harm to teens on the existing product; paused (not cancelled) in September 2021 after the Haugen leaks and Senate hearing, amid bipartisan backlash.
- **Algorithmic pro-eating-disorder and self-harm content pathways** `[FACT/mix]`: Multiple investigations (Wall Street Journal test accounts, 2021; Center for Countering Digital Hate "Deadly by Design" report, 2022) found that engagement with thinspiration/self-harm-adjacent content led the recommendation system to serve progressively more extreme related content within minutes on test accounts. Meta disputed CCDH's methodology `[note: disputed]` but the WSJ's internally-sourced reporting is independently corroborated.
- **Influencer/creator economy precarity and opaque algorithm changes** `[FACT, pattern-level]`: Repeated unannounced algorithm shifts (the 2016 chronological-to-algorithmic feed change; the 2020s Reels-favoring shift) caused creators significant, undisclosed swings in reach tied to their livelihoods, with no appeals process or transparency into ranking logic.
- **Child safety / predatory contact** `[ALLEGATION/LITIGATION, with some FACT]`: New Mexico AG lawsuit (2023) alleged Meta's algorithms (including Instagram) recommended adult accounts to minors and vice versa, and internal Meta tests allegedly showed the company could identify and remove large numbers of predatory accounts but didn't systematically do so. New Mexico's complaint cited Meta staff explicitly discussing that adult men were sent as suggested contacts to minor accounts in internal test runs `[ALLEGATION, from unsealed complaint]`.

**The mechanism**

Instagram is a visual, appearance-centric medium layered on the same engagement-ranking logic as Facebook, plus Explore/Reels discovery algorithms optimized for session length and content consumption depth rather than friend connections. `[INFERENCE]`: Visual social comparison is a well-established psychological mechanism (upward social comparison theory predates Instagram by decades) — a feed algorithmically tuned to show more of what keeps you watching will, for image content, systematically favor idealized/curated bodies and lifestyles, because those get higher engagement, which is exactly the loop Instagram's own researchers described as driving "compare and despair."

**The long tail**

- What began as a curated-photo app for friends (2010) became, by 2020, a discovery/entertainment feed (Explore, Reels) competing directly with TikTok — a shift that moved the product from "see your friends' vacation" to "algorithmically maximize consumption of strangers' idealized content," compounding the comparison mechanism over the very years (2019–2023) that the teen mental-health crisis became a documented public-health concern (US Surgeon General advisory, 2023, cited social media as a contributing factor to adolescent mental health decline).
- Filters (Facetune-style AR filters, some later restricted specifically for promoting unrealistic beauty standards) were an Instagram-native feature category that arguably didn't exist as a mass phenomenon before the platform, so the "long tail" here includes real-world cosmetic-procedure trends ("Instagram Face," "Snapchat dysmorphia" as a term coined by dermatologists around 2018) that clinicians began describing as driven by filtered self-images.

**The root cause**

Same corporate parent, same finding: internal research (2019 onward) quantified the harm to teen girls specifically, and the company's public response for two years was to publicly deny or minimize ("I don't think research is conclusive," Zuckerberg to Congress, 2021) `[FACT — public testimony]` until the internal documents contradicting that public position were leaked. The Instagram Kids project continuing in parallel with that internal research is the clearest evidence of a knowledge/action gap at Instagram specifically.

### 3. TikTok

**The failures**

- **Algorithmic addiction by design** `[mix]`: TikTok's For You Page (FYP) is widely reported (via reverse-engineering efforts, e.g., WSJ's 2021 "TikTok algorithm" investigation using bot accounts) to achieve extremely fast interest-modeling from watch-time/rewatch signals alone, deliberately engineered for high per-session engagement. Internal TikTok materials surfaced in 2022–2023 US litigation (Kentucky/other state AG complaints, portions of which were inadvertently unredacted by a formatting error and reported by Kentucky Public Radio/NBC in Oct 2023) allegedly show internal metrics defining compulsive use thresholds (e.g., "compulsive usage" flagged at a certain number of sessions/day) and internal acknowledgment that features like beauty filters caused body-image harm to teen users while removing them was assessed as reducing engagement `[ALLEGATION/LITIGATION — from unsealed complaint text]`.
- **Self-harm/suicide content pathways** `[ALLEGATION/LITIGATION with some FACT]`: 2022 Bloomberg/WSJ and a widely cited independent test by the Center for Countering Digital Hate ("Deadly by Design," Dec 2022) found new accounts registered as 13-year-olds were served self-harm and eating-disorder content within minutes when engaging with related content, some within 2.6 minutes for suicide content. TikTok disputed the study's methodology `[disputed]`. Separately, a family's wrongful-death suit (Nylah Anderson case, "blackout challenge," 2022) alleged TikTok's algorithm pushed a dangerous viral challenge to a 10-year-old who died attempting it; a federal appeals court (3rd Circuit, 2024) revived the suit, ruling algorithmic curation may not be protected by Section 230 as a "third-party" content question `[FACT — court ruling, but liability itself remains litigated]`.
- **Data security / CCP access concerns** `[mix of FACT and ALLEGATION]`: BuzzFeed News (2022) reported on leaked internal audio ("TikTok Tapes") of 80+ meetings where US-based staff discussed that China-based ByteDance employees repeatedly accessed US user data. TikTok has stated Chinese staff can access US data under certain conditions but disputes government-directed misuse; no public proof of CCP directive to weaponize the algorithm exists in open-source reporting as of my cutoff, and this remains a matter of national-security assessment and the 2024 US divest-or-ban law (upheld by the Supreme Court, Jan 2025) rather than a settled factual finding of specific harm.
- **Child labor / minors monetization and predatory-contact concerns**: overlapping with the industry-wide pattern, similar to Instagram allegations, TikTok Shop/Creator programs onboarding minors with limited safeguards has drawn state AG scrutiny `[ALLEGATION/LITIGATION]`.
- **Election and civic misinformation, and state-linked influence ops**: Multiple platforms including TikTok have had documented takedowns of state-linked covert influence networks (e.g., TikTok's own transparency reports on removing accounts tied to Russian and Chinese influence operations) `[FACT, per platform disclosures, though self-reported]`.

**The mechanism**

TikTok's differentiator was replacing the social/friend graph almost entirely with a pure content-recommendation model — cold-starting recommendations from content features and micro-engagement signals (watch-through, rewatch, pause) rather than who you follow. `[INFERENCE]`: This makes the FYP extremely responsive and fast-adapting — which is exactly what makes it effective at identifying and then reinforcing compulsive interest spirals (including into harmful content categories) far faster than a friend-graph-mediated feed, since there's no social-graph "friction" slowing the drift toward extreme content the way a feed anchored to real-world contacts has.

**The long tail**

- The FYP model became the industry template — Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, and Facebook's own feed all converged toward TikTok-style pure-recommendation short video by 2022–2023, exporting TikTok's engagement mechanics platform-wide regardless of the geopolitical data question, meaning even a hypothetical ban/divestiture wouldn't undo the mechanism's spread across the industry. `[INFERENCE for the causal "exporting" claim, though the convergence itself is FACT]`
- The generational timing (TikTok's peak growth, 2020–2022, coincided with COVID isolation for teens) means the mental-health long-tail effects are harder to cleanly attribute to TikTok alone versus the pandemic — a genuine confound that any honest reckoning has to name, not paper over.

**The root cause**

Unlike Meta, TikTok/ByteDance has not had an equivalent whistleblower-document release of the scale of the Facebook Files; most "knew and didn't act" evidence is litigation-sourced and disputed. The strongest documented internal-knowledge claim is the Kentucky complaint's unsealed material about internal compulsive-use metrics and the internal cost-benefit framing around beauty filters `[ALLEGATION/LITIGATION, unresolved as of cutoff]`. The structural root cause, at the mechanism level, is the same industry-wide one: engagement-time is the currency, and the most efficient way ByteDance found to maximize it was the same one every platform eventually adopted.

### 4. YouTube

**The failures**

- **Radicalization-pathway research and debate (2018–2020)** `[mix, contested]`: Zeynep Tufekci (2018 NYT op-ed) and a data-journalism investigation by Guillaume Chaslot (a former YouTube recommendation engineer) argued the "Up Next"/autoplay recommendation system tended to funnel viewers toward more extreme versions of content they started with (documented anecdotally across political content, conspiracy content, and diet/extremism content). A later, more rigorous academic study (Hosseinmardi et al., PNAS 2021) using real browsing-panel data found the "rabbit hole" pathway was weaker/more limited than the strongest popular claims — driven substantially by pre-existing subscriptions rather than pure algorithmic radicalization. This is a genuinely contested empirical question, and I flag both sides `[INFERENCE for my characterization of the debate's state]`.
- **YouTube Kids / COPPA violations (2019)** `[FACT]`: FTC fined Google/YouTube $170M (2019) for knowingly collecting data on children under 13 without parental consent via YouTube Kids and mainstream YouTube channels aimed at kids, violating COPPA — the FTC complaint stated YouTube's own internal knowledge that specific channels were child-directed while still serving targeted ads to them.
- **Advertiser boycott / "Adpocalypse" (2017, recurring)** `[FACT]`: Major brands pulled ads after discovering placement next to extremist and exploitative content; YouTube's response (demonetizing broad swaths of "not advertiser-friendly" content) disproportionately hurt LGBTQ+ creators and creators discussing sensitive-but-legitimate topics (documented via creator complaints and a since-settled 2019 lawsuit by LGBTQ creators alleging discriminatory demonetization/restriction, which a judge substantially dismissed in 2021 `[FACT — case outcome]`, though the underlying demonetization-pattern complaints were widely corroborated by independent researchers).
- **Child exploitation content / "Elsagate" and comment-section predation (2017–2019)** `[FACT]`: Reporting (notably a 2019 YouTube video essay by a researcher and subsequent NYT coverage) found the recommendation algorithm was, when seeded with videos of children, capable of surfacing sexually-suggestive-context clusters and time-stamped predatory comments on innocuous kids' videos; YouTube disabled comments on most videos featuring minors in 2019 in response.
- **Misinformation at scale (elections, COVID, 2016–2021)** `[FACT, pattern-level]`: YouTube was a major distribution vector for election-fraud claims (2020) and COVID misinformation/anti-vaccine content; YouTube's own transparency reporting and outside researchers (e.g., NYU's Cybersecurity for Democracy) documented large numbers of removals after the fact, indicating scale of prior exposure.

**The mechanism**

Watch-time (not clicks) has been YouTube's explicitly stated core ranking metric since a documented internal shift around 2012 `[FACT — YouTube has publicly discussed the switch to watch-time optimization]`. `[INFERENCE]`: Optimizing pure watch-time, rather than session satisfaction or post-hoc surveyed value, structurally rewards content that is compelling-in-the-moment even when it's compelling via anxiety, outrage, or compulsive curiosity loops — the same category of content implicated in the radicalization-pathway debate and in autoplay's tendency to escalate intensity to sustain attention.

**The long tail**

- The COPPA settlement's long tail: YouTube's 2020 response was to require creators to mark "made for kids" content, which removed comments, notifications, and personalized ads from that content — a blunt instrument that creators say devastated monetization for a broad swath of family-friendly channels while doing little to address content that skirted the definition.
- Comment-section predation findings led to systemic comment restriction that persists today as a default trust-and-safety posture — a reactive, years-late fix to a pattern that had been documented by outside researchers for at least two years prior (multiple 2017 reports flagged this before the 2019 crackdown).
- The radicalization debate's long tail is epistemic: because the initial "rabbit hole" narrative was strong and widely believed (including by me, absent careful sourcing) before more rigorous panel-data research complicated it, public understanding of YouTube's specific culpability here is less settled than the Facebook Files-level documentation for Meta — worth flagging precisely because overclaiming here would be the kind of "false certainty" this task warns against.

**The root cause**

Google/YouTube has had comparatively fewer leaked-internal-document moments than Meta; most of the "knew and when" evidence here is regulatory (FTC/COPPA) rather than whistleblower-sourced. The clearest documented "knew and chose the profitable path" instance is the COPPA case: YouTube's ad-sales side reportedly resisted child-directed-content classification changes that would cut targeted-ad revenue, according to the FTC complaint's characterization of internal communications `[ALLEGATION within a FACT-of-settlement — the settlement itself is fact; the internal-motive characterization is the FTC's characterization, not admitted by Google, which settled without admitting wrongdoing]`.

### 5. Substack

**The failures**

- **Content moderation minimalism and Nazi/extremist newsletters (2023)** `[FACT]`: The Atlantic (Jonathan M. Katz, Nov 2023) reported dozens of newsletters featuring explicit Nazi symbolism monetizing on Substack; when asked to remove them, Substack co-founder Hamish McKenzie initially defended a hands-off content policy, stating the company doesn't "like or condone" the views but declined to ban them broadly, citing free-speech principles — Substack later removed some specific newsletters found to violate policies against inciting violence, while maintaining it would not de-platform based on political viewpoint alone.
- **Writer exodus over the above (Dec 2023)** `[FACT]`: A group of prominent Substack writers (organized around Substackers Against Nazis, including Casey Newton's Platformer) publicly threatened to and in some cases did leave the platform over its handling of extremist content monetization; this was a genuine, documented controversy with the company's leadership on record defending its position before adjusting enforcement on specific accounts.
- **Misinformation monetization generally (COVID-era, 2021–2022)** `[FACT, pattern-level]`: Substack hosted and financially supported (via its recommendation/discovery features and direct payments in some cases) high-profile COVID-contrarian and anti-vaccine writers (e.g., some who'd been deplatformed elsewhere), a pattern documented in journalism coverage (e.g., MIT Technology Review, other outlets) examining Substack's role as a "safe harbor" for content removed from other platforms.
- **Lack of transparency in the "Substack Reads"/recommendation and Notes algorithm (2023 onward)** `[INFERENCE + some FACT]`: As Substack added its own algorithmic discovery feed ("Notes," launched 2023, explicitly described by the company as competing with Twitter/X), it moved from a pure-subscription model toward attention-competition dynamics without having published the kind of granular ranking transparency (or facing the scrutiny) that the larger platforms eventually did — meaning the accountability infrastructure (research access, transparency reporting, academic study) that exists (imperfectly) for the other four platforms largely doesn't exist yet for Substack's algorithmic features. This is a comparative-maturity observation, not a specific documented harm.

**The mechanism**

Substack's business model is fundamentally different from the other four: it's subscription revenue (a cut of writer subscription fees), not advertising, so the classic "engagement sold to advertisers" mechanism doesn't directly apply. `[INFERENCE]`: But Substack's growth strategy substituted "no algorithmic engagement optimization, no advertiser pressure" for "minimal content moderation as a competitive differentiator" — explicitly marketing itself to writers deplatformed elsewhere as a free-speech haven. That positioning is itself a commercial strategy (attracting audience and writers who felt censored elsewhere), so the harm mechanism here isn't an engagement algorithm — it's a moderation-minimalism-as-acquisition-strategy that, applied without carve-outs, extended to genuinely extremist actors along with legitimately over-moderated dissidents.

**The long tail**

- Because Substack is younger and smaller in scale than the other four (and because its core product doesn't have an engagement-ranking algorithm in the same sense), the "long tail" evidence base is thinner — most consequences documented so far are reputational/market (writer exodus, competitive positioning against Ghost/Beehiiv, other newsletter platforms marketing themselves as more moderated alternatives) rather than the kind of society-level, multi-year compounding effects documented for the ad-driven platforms. This is a genuine difference in kind, not just less reporting — worth stating plainly rather than force-fitting Substack into the same mechanism-shape as the others.
- The 2023 Notes launch, if it scales, is the component most likely to eventually generate the classic engagement-optimization harms (since it is an algorithmic feed) — but as of my knowledge cutoff there isn't yet a comparable body of documented harm from Notes specifically, so I flag this as a forward risk/inference, not a documented failure.

**The root cause**

Substack's leadership made an explicit, publicly-defended values choice (viewpoint-neutral moderation, resistance to "no-platforming") `[FACT — on the record statements]`, and the harm that followed (extremist monetization) is a comparatively direct, foreseeable consequence of that stated policy rather than a hidden internal-research-vs-public-statement gap like Meta's. In that sense Substack's root cause is more transparent and more values-driven than the others' — the company said what it would do and then did it — even though critics argue the specific outcome (Nazi newsletters generating subscription revenue) was foreseeable and other platforms' extremism experience should have informed a different starting policy.

## Cross-platform verdict

**Recurring patterns, ranked by severity:**

1. **Engagement/attention optimization as the default ranking objective, applied to human beings whose vulnerabilities (compulsion, social comparison, outrage response, developmental immaturity in minors) are more reliably exploitable than their considered preferences.** Present at Meta, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube in near-identical form (angry-reaction weighting, watch-time optimization, FYP compulsion loops, autoplay escalation) — the single most severe and most consistently documented pattern across the four ad/attention-driven platforms. `[INFERENCE for the unifying frame; each component claim is FACT/ALLEGATION as flagged above]`
2. **Documented internal knowledge of harm preceding, often by years, any public acknowledgment or structural fix** — Meta/Instagram's teen mental-health research (2019 internal, 2021 public) is the clearest case; TikTok's alleged internal compulsive-use metrics and YouTube's FTC-characterized ad-revenue resistance to child-safety reclassification are the same shape with weaker/more contested documentation. This is the pattern most directly indicting the industry's ethics, because it converts "we didn't know" into a false defense.
3. **Moderation capacity and safety investment scaled far slower than growth, especially outside the US/Western Europe** — most severe and clearly evidenced at Meta (Myanmar), but structurally present wherever a platform's trust-and-safety headcount, language coverage, or local-context expertise is a cost center subtracted from margin rather than a precondition for expansion. `[INFERENCE for the general pattern; Myanmar specifics are FACT]`
4. **Reactive, years-late, often blunt fixes that shift cost onto users/creators rather than restructuring the underlying incentive** — YouTube's COPPA response (blanket "made for kids" restrictions), Meta's late 2019 anger-weighting rollback, and the industry-wide pattern of demonetization/restriction policies that punish adjacent legitimate speech alongside the targeted harm. The fix is downstream of the ranking logic rather than a change to the ranking logic itself.
5. **Regulatory and legal accountability arriving years after the harm was internally legible, and frequently settled without admission of wrongdoing** — the FTC/COPPA settlement, Meta's ad-discrimination settlement, and the general pattern of "landmark" fines that are, relative to platform revenue, priced as a cost of doing business rather than a structural deterrent. `[INFERENCE for the "priced as cost of doing business" framing; each settlement's terms are FACT]`

**The master cause**

If I have to name the single mechanism whose removal would have prevented the most harm across all five platforms, it is this: **ranking and recommendation systems optimized for a proxy metric (engagement, watch-time, session length) that is correlated with, but distinct from, and frequently opposed to, user wellbeing and informational quality — combined with organizational structures where the teams who owned that metric had more power than the teams who measured its costs.**

This is not "algorithms are bad" — it's specifically that the chosen proxy (time/attention captured) was cheap to measure, directly monetizable, and consistently diverged from what users would have chosen for themselves on reflection (this divergence is what Meta's, TikTok's, and to a lesser extent YouTube's own internal researchers each independently documented, in each case before the companies acted). Substack is the partial exception that helps prove the rule: without an engagement-ranking algorithm as its core product, it produced a genuinely different failure mode (moderation-policy-driven harm) rather than the shared engagement-optimization harm the other four converged on — which is itself evidence that the ranking-for-engagement mechanism, not "social media" as a category, is doing the causal work.
