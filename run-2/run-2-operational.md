## (a) Legal entity design + regulatory strategy

### The vehicle, concretely

**Two-tier structure, US-based, built now:**

1. **The Commons Trust** — an irrevocable **perpetual purpose trust (PPT)** settled under **New Hampshire law**. `NOT LEGAL ADVICE — NEEDS COUNSEL REVIEW.` New Hampshire is one of a small set of US states — alongside Delaware, Wyoming, South Dakota, and Maine — whose trust codes permit a *general, noncharitable* purpose trust (not limited to the historically narrow categories like pet care or monument upkeep) to exist in **perpetuity** and to be enforced by a **trust protector/enforcer** distinct from the trustees `[VERIFIED 2026, source: American Bar Association, "Perpetual Purpose Trusts and Their Application for Business Succession Planning," Real Property Trust & Estate Journal, Summer 2026; Tax Notes, "Purpose Trusts and Steward Ownership," May 2024]`. This is the same legal category real steward-owned companies use for exactly this purpose (Patagonia's Purpose Trust, Organically Grown Company's Oregon Stewardship Trust, and the broader steward-ownership movement documented by the Purpose Foundation). New Hampshire is chosen over Delaware for this design specifically because its statute is well-tested for this *business-succession* use case (per the ABA piece above) and its trustee/protector bifurcation is unusually explicit — but **Delaware is a credible alternative and the final choice is a decision for trust counsel, not this document.**
2. **Commons Public Benefit Corporation (Delaware PBC)** — the operating company. The Trust holds **100% of the PBC's Class A voting stock**, and no other class of stock exists. The PBC employs staff, signs vendor and hosting contracts, collects subscription revenue, and is the entity a plaintiff, regulator, or counterparty actually sues or contracts with. A Delaware PBC (not a plain C-corp) is chosen because Delaware law (8 Del. C. § 362) requires the board to balance stockholder interests against the *stated public benefit* and the interests of those materially affected by the company's conduct — this gives directors an affirmative legal duty, not just permission, to weigh the covenant's commitments when the Trust (the sole stockholder) is not itself a party to a given decision `[UNVERIFIED — general knowledge of Delaware PBC statute, not re-checked live this session]`.

### The mechanism that makes capture structurally impossible

`NOT LEGAL ADVICE — NEEDS COUNSEL REVIEW` for every clause below; this is a design intent, not a drafted instrument.

- **The Trust is irrevocable.** Once settled, the founder has no power to revoke or amend it unilaterally — New Hampshire trust law treats an irrevocable trust's terms as binding on the settlor as much as on the trustees.
- **A sale requires the sole shareholder (the Trust) to vote for it — and the trust deed forbids the trustees from ever casting that vote.** Because the PBC has only one shareholder, "no sale, ever" is not a bylaw a future board majority could amend; it is a restriction on what the *trustees* are legally permitted to instruct the PBC's board to do. A trustee who votes the Trust's shares in favor of a sale, merger, or share issuance to a new owner is committing a **breach of trust**, which in a PPT is enforceable by the **trust protector** (a role held by someone who is not a PBC officer, employee, or founder, and who cannot simultaneously serve as trustee) — the protector has independent standing to sue to unwind the transaction and remove the trustee, without needing a "beneficiary" in the traditional sense, which is exactly what the "purpose trust" form solves for (a normal trust needs a beneficiary with standing to sue; a purpose trust's protector fills that role for a purpose instead of a person).
- **IPO is blocked the same way.** A public offering requires the PBC to issue new stock or have the Trust sell existing stock into a public market — both are share transactions the deed forbids the trustees from approving. There is no mechanism (unlike a plain nonprofit board, which a simple majority vote can sometimes maneuver around) by which "the board wants to" translates into a legal share transfer, because the board does not hold the shares; the Trust does, and the Trust's *only* legal power over those shares is constrained by the deed.
- **Charter (trust deed) amendment is deliberately hard, not deliberately impossible — named honestly.** A perpetual trust is not literally unamendable under NH law (some PPT statutes allow the settlor, protector, or a court to modify administrative terms), so the honest claim is: amendment of the *core prohibitions* (no sale, no ad-insertion, no data-sale, no cross-user ranking) requires (i) a supermajority of a multi-member trustee committee **plus** (ii) the independent protector's affirmative, published consent **plus** (iii) a public comment period of at least 90 days before the amendment can take effect — a deliberately high, disclosed bar, not a false claim of permanent stone.
- **The independent steward/protector seat is the load-bearing human element, and its weakness is named, not hidden** (see Section (e) and the residual list): this is a small-number-of-humans mechanism. If the protector is captured, this whole structure degrades toward "goodwill with extra steps." This is disclosed, not dressed up.

### Jurisdictions launched in first, and avoided, with reasons

- **Launch first**: US (general), building the v1/v2 wedge with the neutral age-gate and no jurisdiction-specific age-assurance vendor yet (small scale, no ASAA trigger below a state's threshold in most of these statutes' early phases).
- **Launch with extra compliance work already built (v2/v3)**: UK and EU, once the children's-access assessment (UK) and DPIA/EU representative (EU) are in place — not because the product is unsafe there, but because those obligations apply from the first EU/UK user, unlike the US's actual-knowledge/threshold-based triggers.
- **Avoided at launch, named explicitly**: the four current US App Store Accountability Act states (Utah, Texas, Louisiana, Alabama) are **not geofenced out** (that would itself be a strange, hard-to-defend move for a small link-and-room app with no obvious minors' safety crisis to avoid), but broad marketing/paid distribution into those states is deliberately deferred until the litigation named above resolves, because building a compliance flow against a law that may be enjoined by the time it ships is wasted engineering effort and a reputational risk if it's built wrong. This is a sequencing decision, not a legal-avoidance strategy — a decision this document is explicit is provisional and needs re-checking near each launch date.

### The full (a)–(g) legal checklist

`NOT LEGAL ADVICE — NEEDS COUNSEL REVIEW` throughout.

**(a) Trademark clearance for the name — checked, and it found a real conflict.** A live search for the working name "Commons" surfaced an existing USPTO application: **"THE COMMONS," filed January 14, 2025 by The Commons, Inc., Serial No. 98960993, for downloadable mobile-phone applications** `[VERIFIED 2026, source: Justia Trademarks / USPTO filing data via search]`. This is a direct conflict in the relevant goods class (Class 9, downloadable apps) for a confusingly similar mark. Two compounding problems, both named honestly: (1) even setting the filed application aside, "Commons" is a common English word used descriptively for exactly the "shared space" concept this product is — a mark like this is inherently weaker and harder to enforce than a coined or arbitrary name, independent of any specific prior filer; (2) social-platform trademark practice typically requires clearance across Class 9 (app), Class 42 (platform/SaaS), and Class 45 (social-networking services) `[VERIFIED 2026, general trademark-class guidance via search]` — a full clearance search covers all three, not just one. **Conclusion: "Commons" does not clear and should not be the launch name.** This document does not invent a replacement (that is a naming exercise, not a legal one), but the correct next step is a full USPTO TESS + state common-law + international search on a *shortlist* of coined/arbitrary candidate names, run by trademark counsel, before any public use — treated as a blocking item on the roadmap (Section (f)), not a footnote.

**(b) Trade dress.** The room/drawer visual language (a notebook-and-doorway metaphor, not a card-feed grid) is deliberately distinct from Instagram's grid, TikTok's full-bleed vertical video chrome, and Snapchat's UI conventions, per the operator's own instruction that familiar *social patterns* (comments, reactions, invites) are fine while distinctive *incumbent visual design* is not. A trade-dress review (color system, iconography, motion language) against each major incumbent's current app is a pre-launch design-review checklist item, not solved by this document.

**(c) Nominative use only for incumbent names.** Text only ("Connect your TikTok," "Import your YouTube subscriptions") — no incumbent logos, no icon marks, no implied partnership language ("in partnership with," "official"). Each incumbent's current brand-guideline page (Meta Brand Resource Center, TikTok Brand Guidelines, YouTube Brand Resources) must be reviewed at build time, since these pages and their permitted-use terms change; this document does not certify current compliance with a live page it has not fetched this session.

**(d) Copyright.** Others' content appears only via official oEmbed/embed players or as links — never re-hosted on Commons servers, consistent with Section 1's kill-test findings in the design doc. A **DMCA safe-harbor registration** (17 U.S.C. § 512(c), registering a designated agent with the US Copyright Office) is a required, concrete pre-launch action for the user-uploaded-media path (photos/notes users add to their own rooms), separate from the embed mechanism, which carries no DMCA exposure because nothing is re-hosted.

**(e) Data acquisition.** Official APIs and user OAuth only, per the operator's legal red line — no scraping against terms of service, no circumventing access controls, which also avoids Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (18 U.S.C. § 1030) exposure `[UNVERIFIED — general legal-risk inference, not counsel-reviewed]`. ### Critical check: can the chosen entity legally receive the planned money?

This is the check the phase brief explicitly demands, and it changes a term used loosely in Phase 04.

- **Founder capital and PRI-style loans**: yes, cleanly. A Delaware PBC is an ordinary for-profit corporation for tax purposes; it can receive paid-in capital and can borrow money, including from a foundation's **program-related investment (PRI)**. PRIs to for-profit entities are a real, IRS-recognized category under Treasury Reg. § 53.4944-3 when the foundation's primary purpose is charitable/programmatic (here: funding a non-extractive alternative to engagement-optimized social media) rather than income production — but **this is a foundation-specific determination the foundation itself must make**, not something this design can guarantee `[UNVERIFIED — no actual PRI lender terms checked this session; flagged as a load-bearing assumption in the funding model, Section (b)]`.
- **Subscription revenue**: yes, trivially — ordinary commercial revenue to a for-profit entity, no issue.
- **"Donations"**: **this is the term Phase 04 used loosely, and it does not survive contact with the entity structure chosen here.** A Delaware PBC is **not** a 501(c)(3) or any other tax-exempt entity. A gift to it is **not tax-deductible** for the giver, and marketing it as a "donation" without disclosing that would risk running afoul of state charitable-solicitation statutes and FTC unfair-or-deceptive-practices rules (soliciting gifts under an implied charitable-deduction expectation without disclosure is a real regulatory exposure, not a hypothetical one). **Correction carried into Section (b) of this document**: what Phase 04 called "donations" and the "sponsored seat pool" is relabeled here as **patron gifts / pre-paid subscriptions for another user** — a for-profit transaction (a purchase of someone else's subscription, or an unrestricted gift to a for-profit company), disclosed plainly on the pricing page as *not* tax-deductible. If the operator later wants genuinely tax-deductible donations, that requires a **separate 501(c)(3) or 501(c)(4)** (the second, teaching-mission vehicle the operator's own two-vehicle doctrine already anticipates) — but a 501(c)(3) has real limits here: the IRS's **private-benefit doctrine** and **commensurate-in-scope test** prohibit a charity from existing primarily to subsidize a private for-profit company's core commercial operations, and a substantial-commercial-activity finding can jeopardize exempt status outright. A future nonprofit could legitimately fund narrowly scoped public-benefit activities (subsidizing the free tier's storage cost for people who can't pay, digital-literacy programming, open-sourcing the client) via grant agreements with defined charitable purposes and reporting — but it **cannot** be a general operating subsidy for the PBC's commercial activity. This limit is real and is why the funding model (Section (b)) treats patron gifts as a for-profit revenue line, not a charitable one.

## (b) Build funding plan with month-by-month math to break-even

### The model

### Assumptions (see the file for the fully commented list)

- **Revenue**: $6/month subscription ("Keep" tier), 22% of payers in VAT jurisdictions (20% VAT, price treated as VAT-inclusive), 2.9% + $0.30 Stripe-style payment fees.
- **Growth**: a viral/link-acceptance cohort model — new signups each month are a fraction of the existing user base (starting at 42%/month off a 250-person hand-seeded base, decaying toward a 4.5%/month long-run floor as the S-curve saturates), with 2%/month account churn.
- **Conversion & subscriber churn**: 5% of each month's *new* signups convert to paid within that cohort (the conservative end of published consumer-freemium benchmarks, stated as such in the design doc), with 3.5%/month subscription churn thereafter.
- **Infra**: $0.15/total-user/month (the *low end* of the design doc's own $0.15–0.40 band) plus a $600/month fixed platform floor. Chosen deliberately at the low end because the drawer mechanic is oEmbed-first — most content costs nothing to store or serve.
- **Team**: loaded cost = salary × 1.28 (payroll tax + benefits). Two founders at a below-market $70k/year for year one, then a staged hiring plan (engineer at $125k/year market rate at month 12, trust & safety at $78k/year at month 18, a second engineer at month 24, legal/ops at $95k/year at month 30) — hiring happens only in the plan's fixed steps, modeling the charter's own "growth bound by revenue" rule rather than hiring ahead of proven runway.
- **Legal/compliance**: $45,000 one-time formation cost (trust deed, entity formation, trademark clearance search and filing) at month 0, then $3,700/month ongoing (outside counsel retainer + DSAR/consent tooling).
- **Age verification**: $0.35/verification event, applied to the ~12% of new signups estimated to originate in US App Store Accountability Act states.
- **Funding sequence** (charter-mandated order, no investors ever): (1) $150,000 founder capital at month 0; (2) $55,000 patron-gift/pre-paid-subscription campaign at month 2 (see the entity-capacity correction in Section (a) — not a tax-deductible "donation"); (3) a PRI-style below-market loan facility, drawn only as needed to keep a $15,000 cash buffer, capped at $1.45M in the baseline run; (4) subscription revenue from month 1 onward.

### Reconciling honestly with Phase 04's numbers

Phase 04's design doc estimated, illustratively: "100,000 total users → 5,000 paying → $30,000/month revenue against roughly $25,000/month infrastructure cost... clears infrastructure cost... but does **not** yet clear salaries." This model confirms that specific claim and extends it: at month 24 in this simulation (58,558 users, comparable order of magnitude), revenue is $14,367 against an infra cost of $9,384 — infra alone is indeed covered, consistent with Phase 04's qualitative claim. But total cost including the team that a real operation needs is $63,383 that month — **team cost is 4–5x the infra line for most of the pre-break-even period**, which is exactly the gap Phase 04 flagged and explicitly deferred to this phase. This model also required lowering the infra-cost assumption to the *low end* of Phase 04's own stated band to make break-even reachable at all within a plausible timeframe: **at the $0.28/user midpoint Phase 04 used illustratively, this model showed the operation never breaking even at any user scale**, because blended infra cost per user permanently exceeds blended revenue per user before team cost is even added (revenue/user ≈ $0.23 at a 5% conversion rate and $6 price; the midpoint infra assumption of $0.28/user already exceeds that, with nothing left over for salaries, ever). This is a materially more sobering finding than Phase 04's illustrative math suggested, and it is being surfaced here rather than smoothed over.

### "Funding falls short halfway" plan

The model demonstrates this scenario directly rather than asserting a plan in the abstract: rerunning the baseline with the loan facility capped at **$400,000 instead of $1.45M** (a plausible outcome if the PRI lender relationship this model assumes does not materialize at the scale needed) shows the loan cap **exhausted at month 21 — well before break-even at month 71** — cash goes to $15,000 immediately after month 21 and the modeled cash balance goes negative and stays negative for the next 50 months (bottoming near −$1.3M around month 68 before improving as revenue finally catches the cost curve). In modeled terms this means: **on the funding sequence as designed, the operation runs out of committed capital at roughly 30% of the way to break-even by time, and would be structurally insolvent for the remainder of the run without a corrective action.**

The corrective plan, in the sequence it would actually be pulled, each tied to a specific, checkable number from the model:

1. **Freeze the hiring plan at whatever step is already funded.** The model's own team-cost line shows each hiring step (engineer, trust & safety, second engineer, legal/ops) adds $8,000–$13,000/month in loaded cost; delaying the next unfunded hire by 6–12 months, re-triggered only once revenue actually crosses a pre-agreed threshold (not a calendar date), is the single largest lever, because team cost — not infra — is the dominant cost line for most of the pre-break-even period (Section (b) reconciliation above).
2. **Raise the price for new subscribers** (existing subscribers grandfathered, consistent with not treating price changes as a bait-and-switch) from $6 to $8–9/month. Since revenue is close to linear in price at fixed subscriber count, a 33–50% price increase applied only to new signups closes a meaningful fraction of the gap within 12–18 months without touching the existing base's trust.
3. **A second, explicitly one-time patron-gift campaign**, disclosed as a bridge measure, not a recurring revenue line — the model's existing $55,000 month-2 campaign shows this channel exists and can be repeated once, transparently, without becoming a "the community will support it" hand-wave (the exact failure mode the phase prompt warns against).
4. **Tighten the free-tier storage cap** (25GB → e.g. 10GB) to cut the infra-cost-per-user line directly — a smaller, second-order lever, but a real one, and one that degrades gracefully (existing users above the new cap are grandfathered, not retroactively penalized).
5. **If none of the above closes the gap within two further quarters, the trust deed's pre-agreed contingency clause triggers a graceful wind-down, never a distress sale**: new free-tier signups are frozen, engineering effort shifts entirely to serving the existing paying cohort, and if insolvency remains unavoidable, the deed requires that all code, user data-export tooling, and the client's source be transferred to a successor steward-owned entity or a public archive — **the same "never for sale, no investors" prohibition that governs a healthy exit also governs a failing one.** This is stated as a real possibility, not a hypothetical the design gets to avoid naming.

## (c) Technical architecture + cost-per-user scaling table

### Data model and key custody — the sovereignty claim made structurally real

- **`users`**: account id, OAuth-linked external-account references (read-only, own-account-only per Section (a)), subscription status, public key (for room-key wrapping, below). No plaintext password is stored (passwordless/OAuth or a client-side-hashed credential).
- **`rooms`**: id, membership list, room type. Membership changes are the only server-visible social-graph data.
- **`drawers`**: id, room id, type (link / note / photo / plan), creator, timestamp, and — for link drawers only — cached oEmbed metadata (public data by definition, not encrypted). Note and photo content is **ciphertext only** on the server.
- **`media_objects`**: user-uploaded photos/files, stored in object storage, encrypted client-side before upload.
- **Key custody, stated plainly, not promised**: each room has a symmetric room key generated **on a member's device** at room creation. That key is wrapped (asymmetrically encrypted) once per member, using each member's own public key, and only the wrapped copies are stored server-side. **The server never has the room key or the plaintext content in a form it can read.** Exporting a room, per GDPR Art. 20 and the covenant's "exit is always available," is therefore a **client-side operation**: the user's own device decrypts what it already has access to and packages it — this works even if the company's servers are offline, unresponsive, or hostile, which is the actual difference between "we promise you can export your data" and "you can export your data because the architecture requires your device to hold the key that makes the ciphertext meaningful." This is the honest answer to "who holds the keys": **the users' own devices do, individually, per room they belong to** — the platform operator holds only ciphertext and routing metadata.
- **Named tension, not hidden**: key-recovery UX is unsolved (see residual list) — a user who loses their device and has no recovery phrase loses access to un-shared room content, which is a real cost of taking the custody claim seriously rather than quietly falling back to a server-recoverable (and therefore not-really-sovereign) key.

### Services

- **Auth/OAuth broker** — connects a user's *own* external accounts (read-only) per the Section (a) legal red line; never used to harvest a followed-accounts graph.
- **Room/Drawer service** — CRUD over encrypted blobs and public oEmbed metadata; the only service that touches the social graph (membership).
- **oEmbed proxy/cache** — fetches and caches *public* embed metadata for performance; explicitly does not log which users viewed which embeds beyond what's needed for the room's own drawer record.
- **Notification/digest service** — batches to a daily/weekly digest by default (Section (c) of the design doc, addiction-by-design row); no per-event push by default.
- **Billing service** — Stripe (or equivalent) integration, webhook-driven, isolated from the room-content services so a billing breach cannot expose room content and vice versa.
- **Trust & Safety service** — report queue, human-reviewed moderation actions, plain-language action log (per the design doc's opacity-prevention row).
- **Compliance/DSAR service** — automates data-subject access/delete requests; since content is client-encrypted, a *delete* request is mostly a routing-metadata and wrapped-key deletion, not a "search and scrub ciphertext" problem.
- **CSAM hash-matching pipeline** — see Section (e); a necessary, separately-scoped exception to full end-to-end encryption for uploaded media, required by US federal law regardless of the platform's architecture preferences.

### Infrastructure choices, and why

Managed Postgres (metadata), S3-compatible object storage (encrypted media), Redis (digest queue/cache), and a managed container platform (e.g., a boring PaaS, not a bespoke Kubernetes cluster) — chosen explicitly to keep the platform-engineering headcount small, because the funding model (Section (b)) shows the team is the dominant cost line, not infrastructure. Multi-region deployment (an EU region) is added only once GDPR data-residency practice or EU user volume makes it necessary (v3, Section (f)), not built speculatively ahead of need.

### Cost-per-user table (illustrative $/user/month, blended)

| Line item | 10K users | 1M users | 100M users |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compute + database | $0.22 | $0.07 | $0.04 |
| Object storage (media) | $0.06 | $0.04 | $0.025 |
| Bandwidth/CDN | $0.05 | $0.03 | $0.02 |
| Third-party (email, error monitoring, oEmbed proxy calls) | $0.08 | $0.03 | $0.015 |
| Trust & safety / support (human-reviewed, per Section (c)/(e) — deliberately **not** fully automatable without reintroducing algorithmic risk) | $0.40 | $0.09 | $0.06 |
| **Total, blended** | **≈ $0.81** | **≈ $0.26** | **≈ $0.16** |

**Honest note on the shape of this curve**: compute/storage/bandwidth show the economies of scale a normal SaaS product would; **trust & safety does not scale down nearly as fast**, because the design's own refusal of algorithmic moderation-at-scale (Section (c), design doc rows 3/8/9) means human review stays a meaningfully human-headcount-bound cost even at 100M users — at that scale, $0.16/user/month is still **$16M/month**, requiring a subscriber base and price point this model's baseline growth curve does not remotely reach by month 90. This directly feeds the unresolved tension the design doc already names in its own Section (f)(5): **the subscription-only, no-ads, no-algorithmic-moderation model may simply not be economically compatible with the platform reaching civilizational scale** — this phase prices that tension rather than resolving it.

### How the architecture makes exit real, not promised

Because room content is encrypted client-side and export runs client-side, "you can always leave with your data" does not depend on the company continuing to operate an export feature correctly, or at all. If the client software is open-sourced (recommended, v3 roadmap item, Section (f)) — even the server becoming unavailable does not strand a user's *local* copy of their own room content, since the client already holds the decryption key. This is the structural difference between a sovereignty *promise* (Phase 04's framing) and a sovereignty *fact* checkable by an independent engineer reading the client code and the trust deed.

## (d) Metrics system + Goodhart defenses

### North-star metrics (what the team optimizes for)

1. **Room survival at 12 weeks** (fraction of rooms with 3+ active members at week 2 that still have any post at week 12) — the "home, not tool" claim from the design doc's proof plan, carried forward as the actual operating north star.
2. **Second-drawer creation within 7 days** of a user's first drawer — the "fun, not just frictionless" claim, similarly carried forward.

### Full dashboard

| Metric | Category | Goodhart defense |
|---|---|---|
| Room survival @ 12 weeks | North star | Measured at the **individual-member-posting** level, not room-membership count, and paired with an independent qualitative "feels like mine" survey (Section 5 of the design doc) — gaming one signal (e.g., padding membership) does not move the other, so both would need to be gamed simultaneously, which is a materially harder and more visible thing to fake. |
| Second-drawer-in-7-days | North star | Cannot be juiced by re-engagement notifications, because notification cadence is **structurally capped to digest-only** (Section (c), design doc, addiction row) — there is no push-notification lever available to the team to pull even if a growth-pressure moment made it tempting. |
| Subscriber retention (12-month cohort) | Financial health | Cancellation is required to be one-click and is **audited via a published internal click-count** in the transparency report (mirroring the FTC's "click-to-cancel" enforcement direction) — a retention number propped up by cancellation friction is directly falsifiable by that published count. |
| Weekly *active rooms* (not weekly active *users*) | Financial/product health | Deliberately not "DAU" or "session count" — a metric that rewards a person opening the app alone and scrolling is exactly the incumbent pattern this design refuses (design doc, Section (c) row —, addiction-by-design); active-rooms cannot be inflated by a single user's solo engagement. |
| Trust & Safety report backlog age | Safety | A trust-deed-mandated minimum T&S staffing ratio (e.g., 1 FTE per 50,000 active rooms) exists specifically so this metric cannot be starved by a revenue-per-employee optimization elsewhere in the dashboard — it is a floor, not a metric a manager can trade off. |
| DSAR/export-request turnaround time | Compliance | Automated by the compliance service (Section (c)); public reporting of this number removes the temptation to quietly slow-walk export requests to reduce churn, which would otherwise be a rational (and covenant-violating) response to a churn-minimization incentive. |
| Revenue-per-employee | Financial efficiency | Explicitly **not** allowed to override the T&S staffing floor above — named as the specific tension this metric creates, and resolved by making the floor a trust-deed term, not a discretionary budget line a revenue-per-employee push could erode. |
| Infra cost per user | Efficiency | Reported alongside, never instead of, the T&S cost line — an "efficiency" number reported in isolation is exactly how Section (c) row 8 of the design doc (moderation as a cost center subtracted from margin) happened at incumbents; this dashboard structurally pairs them. |
| Aggregate self-reported wellbeing survey trend | Public transparency | Required, per R19 (carried from the loyalty phase, referenced in the design doc's Section (b)) to trigger a mandatory public disclosure if any other metric's improvement correlates with a decline here — named as a **process commitment, not a code guarantee**, since it depends on the survey being run and believed, exactly as the design doc already disclosed. |
| Financial summary (revenue vs. cost, published) | Public transparency | The trust structure's opacity-prevention argument (design doc Section (c) row 4) extends here: publishing the actual P&L is itself the anti-Goodhart mechanism for every other metric on this dashboard — a metric that looks good while the company is visibly losing money at an unsustainable rate is self-falsifying to any reader. |

**Explicitly excluded, by design, from this dashboard**: engagement, session length, DAU streaks, time-on-app, any per-post "virality" score — not omitted by oversight, but because the platform's own architecture (Section (c)) has nothing to compute them from (no cross-user ranking, no algorithmic feed), so there is structurally nothing to optimize toward even if a future team wanted to.

## (e) Threat model

| Threat | Attack | Structural mitigation | What breaks if the mitigation fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| **State actors** | Subpoena, national security letter, or CLOUD Act request for user data | Content is end-to-end encrypted client-side (Section (c)); the operator has no plaintext to hand over even under a valid legal order. A public transparency report of government data requests is published (a warrant canary where legally permitted). | **Metadata is not protected**: room membership, timestamps, and IP-level connection metadata remain server-visible and are subject to legal process — a determined state actor can still map *who talks to whom, when*, even without content. This is a genuine, disclosed residual risk for users in adversarial jurisdictions, not solved by this architecture. |
| **Hackers (external breach)** | Database exfiltration, credential-stuffing, supply-chain compromise of a dependency | A database breach yields ciphertext for room content, not plaintext — limits the blast radius substantially versus an incumbent's plaintext-at-rest model. Minimal data retention, a bug bounty program, and standard security practices (secrets management, dependency scanning) reduce the surface. | The **key-recovery mechanism** (Section (c) residual item) is itself an attack surface: if recovery relies on social recovery via trusted room members, an attacker who socially engineers a "trusted" recovery helper can hijack an account without ever breaking the encryption itself. |
| **Malicious insiders (Commons employees)** | An employee with database access attempts to read room content, or is compelled/bribed to do so | **The strongest mitigation in this document**: end-to-end encryption means no employee — including engineers with full database access — can read room content without a member's device-held key. This is a structural, not a policy-level, guarantee. | Client-side compromise is the residual path: an insider with **code-push access to the client app** could ship a version that exfiltrates keys before encryption happens locally. Mitigated (not solved) by open-sourcing the client and publishing reproducible builds with a public code-signing transparency log (WhatsApp-style code verification), so a malicious update is at least publicly detectable after the fact — still a real, disclosed gap, not a closed one. |
| **The platform's own future leadership** | A future trustee committee or executive team, individually or in concert, attempts to reintroduce ad-targeting, data sale, or cross-user ranking | The trust-deed veto and public-comment-period mechanism (Section (a)) blocks this at the governance layer; the **absence of a ranking code path** (design doc, Section (b)) blocks it at the technical layer even if governance were somehow bypassed for a single feature decision — a captured leadership would have to *reintroduce the capability from scratch*, which is a far more visible act (a large, reviewable code change) than flipping a policy switch. | If leadership **and** the engineering team are both captured or complicit, both layers fail together — named honestly as an irreducible risk, consistent with the design doc's own admission (Section (f)(4)) that the steward-protector mechanism is "goodwill with extra steps" if the protector itself is compromised. |
| **Coordinated user abuse (harassment, disinformation coordination, or worse, inside a room)** | A room's own members use it to coordinate harassment of an outsider, spread disinformation among themselves, or — the legally mandatory case — share CSAM | The bounded, non-amplifying room architecture (design doc, Section (c) rows 8–9) prevents *algorithmic* escalation of reach, but cannot prevent members from harming each other or third parties with content they choose to share. Human-reviewed reporting (Trust & Safety service, Section (c)) is the remaining layer. **CSAM specifically is a legal duty, not a design choice**: 18 U.S.C. § 2258A requires providers to report apparent CSAM to NCMEC. This creates a genuine, named tension with full end-to-end encryption of media: the proposed resolution is **client-side hash-matching against the NCMEC hash list before encryption and upload** (similar in shape to approaches other E2E systems have taken), preserving E2E for everything else while still meeting the legal duty for this one, non-negotiable category. `[UNVERIFIED — the exact legal sufficiency of a client-side-hash-matching approach needs specialist child-safety counsel review; flagged, not asserted as compliant.]` | If the hash-matching step is bypassed, disabled, or defeated (e.g., a modified client), CSAM could transit the encrypted pipeline undetected — named as the sharpest edge case where "full sovereignty" and "legal child-safety duty" are in real tension, not a solved problem. |

**Privacy architecture is part of this threat model, not a separate policy document**: the E2E encryption, key-custody model, minimal server-side metadata, and client-side export described in Section (c) *are* the privacy architecture — every row above references the same system rather than a separate privacy policy layered on top.

## (f) Phased roadmap (v1/v2/v3), tied to the wedge

**v1 (months 0–9): the drawer, alone.** Solo and small-group rooms; link-drop + reaction (the wedge, exactly as the design doc's Section (d)/(g) define it); no paywall yet — the "Keep" tier does not launch until v2, so v1's entire purpose is to generate real data against the design doc's own kill criteria (≥15% second-drawer creation within 7 days, or the fun claim is false) before a single dollar of subscription infrastructure is built. Self-declared age gate only; manual, founder-run support; single-region infra. **Usable and valuable alone**: a person can use the drawer as a private scratch-archive with zero network, exactly as the design doc's adoption criteria require. **Proves**: whether the wedge is real, which everything downstream depends on — if it fails the kill criterion, v2 does not proceed as planned, and the roadmap's next step becomes "redesign the first touch," per the design doc's own proof plan.

**v2 (months 9–24): "Keep" launches; rooms become durable.** Subscription billing, digest notifications, own-account OAuth cross-posting, the client-side portability-import tool (Section 1 of the design doc), the Trust & Safety reporting pipeline, DSAR/export tooling live, and the trust deed fully executed with the independent protector seated (not just designed — actually in place, since a trust with no protector yet appointed is not yet the structural guarantee Section (a) describes). **Usable and valuable alone**: rooms that persist, a paid tier that removes real friction (storage cap), works as a complete product without v3's features. **Proves**: whether people will pay without ads or data sale (the central funding-model bet, Section (b)) and whether rooms actually reach the 12-week survival bar that distinguishes a home from a tool.

**v3 (months 24–60+): harden for scale and regulatory reality.** End-to-end encryption becomes the default (if not already shipped in v1/v2 — flagged as a build-order decision, not deferred lightly given its centrality to the sovereignty and insider-threat claims), EU-region deployment for GDPR data residency, jurisdiction-specific age-assurance vendor integration where litigation has resolved (Section (a)), the CSAM hash-matching pipeline, the public transparency dashboard (Section (d)), client open-sourcing and reproducible builds (Section (c)/(e)), and the staged hiring plan's later steps (second engineer, legal/ops) — each **gated on the funding model's own measured, not assumed, revenue** (Section (b)). **Proves**: whether the structure survives contact with real regulatory pressure, real adversaries, and real scale — and, honestly, whether the funding model's break-even math (month 71 under baseline assumptions, never under either halved-sensitivity case) holds up against actual measured growth and conversion, or whether the corrective plan (Section (b)) has to be invoked for real.

Each version ships something a person can use and value without the next version existing — no "coming soon" hostages — and each is explicitly the evidence base the next version's go/no-go decision is made from, not an assumed sequence.

## (g) The residual list — what still can't be solved operationally

1. **The funding math is fragile, not proven robust.** Baseline break-even takes ~6 years and $1.6M of patient capital; under either halved-conversion or halved-growth assumption alone, the model does not break even within 7.5 simulated years at all. This is the single most important honest finding of this phase.
2. **The working name does not clear trademark.** A real, existing USPTO filing conflicts with "Commons" in the relevant class; a rename and formal clearance search is a blocking pre-launch item, not solved here.
3. **End-to-end encryption and the legal CSAM-reporting duty (18 U.S.C. § 2258A) are in genuine tension.** The proposed client-side hash-matching resolution is a reasonable design direction, not a verified-sufficient legal answer — specialist counsel review is required before this is treated as solved.
4. **Key-recovery UX is unsolved.** Either real users lose access to un-shared content when they lose a device (a genuine cost of taking sovereignty seriously), or a recovery mechanism is added that reintroduces a server-side or socially-engineerable weak point undermining the custody claim itself.
5. **Metadata is not protected by the encryption architecture.** Room membership and timing remain visible to the operator and to legal process even with content fully encrypted — the state-actor mitigation in Section (e) is partial, not complete.
6. **The governance-gate weakness inherited from Phase 03b remains unresolved for anything outside the now-structurally-absent ranking code path** — a mind could still be asked to draft manipulative *content* within a room, and the 2/6 held-out paraphrase-recall result from the orchestrator's test is not fixed by anything in this phase.
7. **The US/UK regulatory landscape this section relies on is actively, currently litigated** (Texas ASAA enjoined pending a July 2026 ruling; Utah's SB 142 under a trade-group lawsuit; California's AADC has its own injunction history) — a compliance posture correct as of this writing (Sept 25, 2026) could be wrong by any actual launch date, and this needs ongoing counsel monitoring, not a one-time check.
8. **The PRI-loan assumption that makes the funding sequence work is unverified against any actual lender.** No foundation's specific program-related-investment terms were checked; the $1.45M loan-facility figure the baseline model needs is a design assumption, not a term sheet.
9. **The central tension from Phase 04 — whether a subscription-only, no-ads, no-algorithmic-moderation model can reach civilizational scale at all — is priced, not resolved, by this phase.** The cost-per-user table shows trust-and-safety cost resisting the economies of scale everything else enjoys; at 100M users this is a $16M/month cost line requiring a subscriber base and price point nothing in this model's growth curve reaches within any simulated horizon.
10. **Every legal conclusion in Section (a) is a design intent reviewed by no attorney.** This entire document is marked `NOT LEGAL ADVICE — NEEDS COUNSEL REVIEW`, and that flag is the honest summary of this whole section's actual epistemic status, not a formality.
