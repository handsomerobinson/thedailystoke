Blueprint Final 0 substantive changes in the last round

Take away the machine, not the feature. Then hand people one standing link.

A multi-agent design tournament asked how to build a social platform that cannot turn on its users. Three full runs, a build round and two charter-drafting rounds converged on one answer: a calm, free tool for groups that meet in person again and again, owned by a structure that no one can sell or quietly reverse.

The diagnosis

Every big platform runs the same machine

The harms at Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube and X trace back to four parts working together. Substack is the control case: it had no ad feed and still drifted back to algorithmic discovery once venture growth demanded it. Fixing one feature leaves the machine running. The design cuts every part.

An objective that rewards captured attention

Cut: no optimization target over people. No feed, no ranking.

A funder whose return grows with that capture

Cut: no investor, no creditor who can seize the service.

Internal research that measured the harm

Cut: pre-registered research, published within 12 months.

Governance nobody affected could force

Cut: five locks, and quiet reversal made impossible.
What gets built

A better routine by week two, not a better first night

The organiser of a book club, support group, tenant association, mutual-aid crew or library programme pastes one standing link into the chat the group already uses. Anyone can open it in a browser, with no account and no app, tap “I’m in”, and see when, where, who’s coming and who’s bringing what.

Private content is encrypted so the operator can’t read it. Everything exports, and anyone can run their own copy. It only beats Apple, WhatsApp and Partiful for recurring groups that span phone platforms, or whose members shouldn’t have to expose a phone number or account.

Who owns it

No one can sell it, and weakening it takes five locks

A Delaware nonprofit runs the service. Its only voting member is an irrevocable purpose trust whose deed forbids voting for any weakening change. A separate Veto Foundation holds the name, domains and code rights. A law-school clinic holds a second veto and can pause a risky release for at most 60 days on its own. Users who hold credentials choose 2 of the 5 directors. If the money runs out, data is exported and deleted before any bankruptcy filing, and nothing is ever sold.

  1. 90 days of public notice
  2. Two-thirds of the board (4 of 5)
  3. The purpose trust
  4. The Veto Foundation, 4 of 5
  5. A vote of the users themselves

The money

A donor-funded public utility, indefinitely

Users pay what they want, organisations can pay for paperwork features, and institutions can support it without getting perks, data or influence. The comparison with Signal settled it: earned revenue alone will never cover the costs.

The optional AI guide

A reference mind that can’t be turned against its user, and doesn’t ship yet

The reference mind in reference-mind/ passes 243 tests and a 30-check demo with no API key. It never sends other people’s words off the device. Its loyalty charter can only be removed by the person it serves. The red team’s seven attacks went from winning 7 of 7 to 0 of 7.

But its word-matching guard catches only 17 of 32 held-out attacks, and adding more rules made no difference: the build round missed the same 15. No run has tested a real model. The guide ships only after a real model passes an independently written battery of at least 500 cases.

What the tournament killed

Ideas that didn’t survive the red teams

Killed

The “view from above” wedge. A calm layer over your existing TikTok, Instagram and YouTube feeds was the operator’s own hypothesis. The platforms’ APIs and the law don’t allow it.

Withdrawn

“Earned revenue covers costs around year 11.” Rerun at Signal’s 0.2% giving rate, earned revenue never covers costs, and cash goes negative at month 118.

Rejected

Creator economics. No creator fund, revenue share or payouts. The money only funds building and running the service.

Not enough

More keyword rules for the AI guard. They added no catches on held-out attacks. Only a real model with a semantic guard can close the gap.

What remains

Ten gates that no amount of writing can close

Next, in this order: money, then counsel, then the pilot. H4 decides whether any of it was worth building.

  1. Money

    $2.3M in signed, non-controlling commitments, checked by an accountant.

  2. Counsel

    Answers to 23 questions on the trust, board, bankruptcy, tax and privacy. An IRS determination by month 18.

  3. Name clearance

    “Stoke”, “The Commons” and “Porchlight” all fail trademark clearance.

  4. Organiser pilot

    40 organisers over 12 weeks, then the H4 study with a waitlist control.

  5. Institutions

    Letters of intent; 25 supporting institutions by month 18.

  6. A real-model mind

    A held-out battery of 500+ attacks plus 100 benign and 200 injection cases, run on the shipped model.

  7. Real trust roots

    Passkeys, an external transparency log and release-key signatures replace the stand-ins.

  8. Security

    An independent crypto audit, VM-grade isolation, a canary red team and reproducible builds.

  9. Jurisdiction

    A UK Online Safety Act assessment, EU tripwires and geoblocking where age checks are mandatory.

  10. Outside review

    Recommended, not required: review by a non-Claude model and by humans.

Caveats

Every phase and every judge was a Claude model. Some of the convergence may reflect one model family’s taste.

No LLM API key was available, so the AI guide has never been tested with a real model.

Blind phases were kept blind by instruction and separate directories, not a sandbox. One contaminated launch in Run 3 was stopped, deleted unread and relaunched, and that is disclosed in its run card.

The charter was drafted by AI and hasn’t been reviewed by a lawyer. The design converged on paper only.
